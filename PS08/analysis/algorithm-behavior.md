# Peterson, LL/SC spin lock, and MCS: qualitative behavior

Research notes for the PS08 teaching presentation. These are algorithm and workload analyses, not native timing results. PDF page references below use printed page numbers unless a PDF viewer page is explicitly given.

## Correctness and progress

Use separate terms: **mutual exclusion** means at most one thread in the critical section; **deadlock-freedom** means some pending request eventually enters; **starvation-freedom** means every pending request eventually enters. Progress claims assume required threads eventually execute, critical sections terminate, and participants do not crash. They are not elapsed-time guarantees. Peterson's original paper distinguishes mutual exclusion from deadlock/lockout and proves the latter by considering the other participant's behavior. [Peterson 1981, pp.115–116](https://zoo.cs.yale.edu/classes/cs323/doc/Peterson.pdf)

- **Peterson:** two participants announce intent with `flag[i]`, then give the other priority with `turn`. If both want entry, the last `turn` write breaks the tie. Under atomic reads/writes in a sequentially consistent execution, mutual exclusion and progress hold. Analysis of the original protocol gives bounded waiting: after a participant completes its intent/turn doorway, its peer can enter at most once before yielding priority on its next attempt. This bounds overtaking, not how soon the scheduler runs the waiting thread. [Original algorithm and proof, pp.115–116](https://zoo.cs.yale.edu/classes/cs323/doc/Peterson.pdf)
- **Peterson memory caveat:** the loads/stores proof assumes sequential consistency. Unqualified code can fail under relaxed memory consistency; executable versions require suitable atomics and ordering. [OSTEP, Peterson sidebar, p.7](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/OSTEP/threads-locks.pdf#page=7)
- **LL/SC lock:** LL reads the flag; when zero, SC attempts to set one. Only successful SC grants entry; unlock stores zero. Without a queue, a contender can repeatedly lose and starve. Deadlock-freedom needs eventual successful retries after release. This is a spin lock, not a lock-free algorithm. [OSTEP §28.10, pp.11–12, Figs.28.5–28.6](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/OSTEP/threads-locks.pdf#page=11)
- **Arm exclusives:** exclusive monitors track a reservation granule, not just the numerical value; competing updates can invalidate the reservation. A context switch resets the local monitor and can cause a false-negative SC failure even without a competitor changing the flag. Failure alone does not prove another thread acquired the lock. Retry from LL. Correctness requires ordering of protected accesses as well as atomic ownership; the Arm examples place DMB after acquisition and before release. For progress, assume a valid exclusive loop and opportunities to complete it without repeated reservation loss; do not promise that every contender's next SC succeeds. Arm recommends keeping the pair close and avoiding intervening data accesses/cache maintenance. Arm `STREX` status zero means success, whereas OSTEP's abstract SC returns one for success. [Arm DHT0008A §1.2.1–1.2.3, pp.1-4–1-8; mutex example, pp.1-12–1-13](https://documentation-service.arm.com/static/5e9415a4c8052b1608762858#page=8)
- **MCS:** atomic tail exchange establishes queue order; each waiter links its node to its predecessor and spins on its own `locked` field. Release clears the successor's field, or CAS resets an empty queue's tail. Standard MCS is FIFO in successful enqueue-exchange order, not wall-clock call-start order. If each predecessor eventually runs and releases, later requests cannot overtake an enqueued waiter. The original release variant without CAS loses strict FIFO. [Mellor-Crummey & Scott 1991 §2.4, pp.29–33; Fig.5 p.30](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/scott/papers/1991_TOCS_synch.pdf#page=9)

## Workload cases for slides

| Case | What to explain |
|---|---|
| Uncontended | Peterson writes intent/turn and tests its peer. LL/SC performs a successful pair. MCS initializes a node, swaps the tail, and CAS clears an empty queue on release. These operation paths do not establish a universal latency ranking. |
| Many waiters on multiple CPUs | LL/SC waiters share a flag; cached reads need not all be remote. Release updates that line and contenders race. MCS waiters spin on distinct local nodes; a predecessor signals its successor. |
| One CPU or more runnable threads than CPUs | A descheduled holder stalls all three locks. Waiters spend CPU spinning until it runs and releases; a spinner monopolizing the sole CPU can prevent that. |
| Preempted MCS successor | Even after release, later running waiters remain behind a descheduled queue successor. Also, a successor preempted after exchanging the tail but before linking `next` can make its predecessor spin inside unlock. |
| Short vs. long critical section | Spinning can be reasonable when expected waiting is shorter than blocking/wakeup overhead and the holder runs concurrently. Longer waiting or holder preemption increases wasted CPU; blocking or adaptive waiting may help. MCS reduces communication while waiting, but still uses CPU to spin. |

The uncontended operation paths follow [Peterson Fig.1, p.115](https://zoo.cs.yale.edu/classes/cs323/doc/Peterson.pdf), [OSTEP Fig.28.6, p.12](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/OSTEP/threads-locks.pdf#page=12), and the [Rochester MCS pseudocode, “The MCS list-based queue lock”](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/research/synchronization/pseudocode/ss.html). Shared-flag behavior is analysis of the LL/SC loop together with the [MCS paper's contention discussion, §2.1–2.4, pp.24–33](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/scott/papers/1991_TOCS_synch.pdf#page=4). MCS guarantees **O(1) remote references per acquisition**, not constant total waiting time; locality assumes appropriate node placement and caching. [MCS §2.4, p.29](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/scott/papers/1991_TOCS_synch.pdf#page=9)

Single-CPU/preempted-holder behavior is described in [OSTEP §28.8, pp.9–10](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/OSTEP/threads-locks.pdf#page=9). Successor preemption follows directly from the [MCS acquire/release pseudocode](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/research/synchronization/pseudocode/ss.html), and is explicitly identified as a problem for deterministic locks in [Kontothanassis, Wisniewski & Scott 1997, §2, pp.5–6; §4.1, p.11](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/scott/papers/1997_TOCS_synch.pdf#page=3). Temporary preemption is a scheduling stall, not by itself proof of an algorithmic deadlock; permanent loss of a required participant breaks the progress assumptions.

The short/long-wait tradeoff follows [Scheduler-Conscious Synchronization §1, p.4](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/scott/papers/1997_TOCS_synch.pdf#page=2). Its numerical context-switch heuristic is historical, not a universal threshold for current systems.

## Claims to avoid

- Do not say “MCS is always fastest” or “Peterson is always slowest.” Consider contention, core count, scheduling, critical-section duration, cache layout, memory ordering, and the actual implementation. The MCS paper separately evaluates uncontended latency and scaling. [§4.3, latency discussion/Table I, pp.50–51](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/scott/papers/1991_TOCS_synch.pdf#page=30)
- Native x86 CAS-loop timings are not measured LL/SC timings: equivalent ownership protocols do not reproduce exclusive-monitor behavior. [OSTEP §28.9–28.10, pp.10–12](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/OSTEP/threads-locks.pdf#page=10)
- Do not equate FIFO or bounded waiting with scheduler fairness, nor deadlock-freedom with starvation-freedom. State the assumptions alongside the guarantees.
